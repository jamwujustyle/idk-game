# The Drowned Road

Original narrative proposal. Working title; complete story spoilers below.

## The game in one paragraph

Twelve years ago, a swordsman closed an escape gate while his commander flooded a valley to win a war. Now drought has uncovered the valley, and the government is arresting people who officially died there. When the swordsman refuses to execute one of those survivors, he becomes a fugitive in the country he helped create. His former comrades hunt him through abandoned villages, mountain shrines, and fields growing inside the ruins. As the rains return, he must open the road he once closed—and protect people who have every reason to hate him.

The central question is: **What does taking responsibility demand when forgiveness is not yours to ask for?**

## Setting and tone

The fictional province of Kurose draws on the landscapes, material culture, and sword traditions of premodern Japan. It is an invented place with its own political history. The story makes no claim to reconstruct a particular historical period.

Kurose is a river province. Its terraces, ferries, grain stores, villages, and fortresses depend on the management of water. Twelve years earlier, General Iori ended a civil war by flooding the lower valley and trapping the opposing army. Civilians trapped with it were recorded as enemy auxiliaries or omitted from the account. The reservoir subsequently became essential to the farms downstream. A real public benefit rests on an unacknowledged crime.

After two dry years, much of the reservoir bed is exposed. Some survivors have returned to recover their homes and family remains; others never left the surrounding hills. New settlers farm the mud between submerged rooftops. People the state considers dead have built an imperfect, living community.

The landscape begins in late summer: insects, pale reeds, bright sky, cracked mud, blue cloth drying over ruined walls. Autumn brings red leaves, woodsmoke, and clouds gathering beyond the mountains. Rain changes familiar paths into channels during the final act.

The world should also be pleasurable to inhabit. A cook experiments with recipes reconstructed from memory. Children turn a stranded boat into a theatre. Two old neighbors renew an argument over a boundary now marked by the same tree growing through both houses. These people have lives beyond what was done to them.

## The people

**Ren — the player character.** About forty, a court executioner and former military swordsman. Precise, reserved, capable of dry humor. He believes a clean death is the last kindness his work permits. This belief has also allowed him to continue serving a violent institution without questioning who it condemns. He knows civilians were behind the gate he closed. The game never reveals that he was secretly innocent.

Ren wants to confess, settle his debt, and perhaps die doing something worthy. The survivors need him to do less satisfying things: keep a route open, carry supplies, accept distrust, and remain alive to answer questions. His arc moves from seeking a personal ending to accepting an ongoing obligation.

**Aya — Ren's younger sister.** A river surveyor in her thirties who organizes the valley settlements. She survived the flood through a maintenance channel and deliberately never sought her brother out. She knows what he did. She has spent years finding water, repairing crossings, and helping families live under borrowed identities. Her community is her life's work, rather than a temporary refuge awaiting Ren's arrival. She will accept his help without offering reconciliation as payment.

**General Iori — the principal antagonist.** Ren's former commander and teacher, now the governor. He built roads, repaired irrigation, and sustained a long peace after his victory. He is sincerely committed to preventing another famine or war. He also considers himself entitled to decide whose losses can be omitted from that peace.

Iori's immediate objective is to refill the reservoir when the rains return, securing water for the next planting season. Restoring the reservoir does require relocation from its exposed bed. His crime is making safe relocation conditional on surrendering the survivors' identities, testimony, and property claims—and eventually using soldiers against those who refuse. There is a workable alternative: evacuate everyone, acknowledge the original villages, preserve their claims, and investigate the flood. He rejects it because it threatens his authority and the estates awarded to his officers.

**Masato — Ren's former pupil.** A young officer who believes lawful service protects ordinary people from whoever happens to have the strongest sword. He knows Ren as the man who insisted that discipline meant restraint. Ren's flight looks like hypocrisy and betrayal. Masato's question is whether obeying the law still serves people when the authorities knowingly falsify who those people are.

**Daigo — an old comrade.** A road captain who has secretly protected survivors with false papers for years. He did good work inside the system while Ren looked away. He fears Ren's sudden need for honesty will expose families who are currently safe. He is right about that danger. Helping him means protecting people's choice to remain anonymous.

**Hana — the first person Ren saves.** A carpenter in her late fifties, arrested after petitioning for her old home under her own name. The court calls this impersonating a dead woman. She is practical, occasionally funny, and entirely unimpressed by Ren's anguish. She becomes instrumental in preparing the evacuation crossings.

**Ema — a survivor leading an armed faction.** She helped defend the settlements when official protection failed. Now she intends to destroy the irrigation headworks and devastate the estates downstream. Tenants and laborers would suffer alongside Iori's allies. Her anger has a clear cause; the people she would kill have not consented to be its price.

## Opening: the woman who is already dead

The first image is water falling into a stone basin. A hand rinses a sword. Beyond a wall, festival drums mark the anniversary of the peace.

The player takes control in a shrine courtyard. Ren prepares for an execution while Masato insists on one last practice exchange before the ceremony. Their brief spar introduces movement, guard, and counters through familiar banter. Ren corrects him: a fighter who rushes the finish stops watching the person in front of him.

Hana is brought into the courtyard. Her offense is read aloud: she has claimed the name, home, and compensation belonging to a woman dead for twelve years. She says the name is her own.

Ren notices a piece of carved doorframe hanging from her neck. It bears the mark of his childhood village. He asks where she found it.

“On my house. Where I left it.”

When he asks how she survived, she recognizes him.

“You shut the gate. You didn't stay to count.”

Ren knows precisely which gate she means. For years he has treated the flood as something finished that no action could repair. Now he is being asked to make its false death count true, one living person at a time. He lowers his sword and asks for the execution to be stayed. The presiding officer refuses: Iori has already confirmed the sentence.

Ren cuts Hana's bindings.

Masato blocks the courtyard exit. He initially asks Ren to surrender, because walking away with a condemned prisoner will make both of them fugitives. Ren cannot trust a hearing under the man who ordered the sentence. Their practice becomes the first real duel.

The player wins through the combat system. Ren disarms Masato and leaves him alive. Hana opens the outer gate while they fight; she participates in her own escape.

Beyond the shrine, they descend toward a lake that is no longer there. Roofs protrude from grass. Smoke rises from a village the official map marks as submerged.

Hana says someone there will want to see him. She does not promise it will be a happy reunion.

**Title: THE DROWNED ROAD.**

## Act I: what survived

Ren reaches the settlements and finds Aya alive. Their reunion is quiet. She looks at his sword before she looks at his face.

“You kept it.”

The original crime becomes explicit during this act. In a short, playable memory, Ren stands at the escape gate while water begins filling the lower streets. He receives the order to seal the crossing against enemy soldiers. There are civilians in the crowd. He sees them. He obeys. The scene establishes a past action, rather than presenting the player with a false opportunity to rewrite it.

In the present, Aya allows Ren to stay because Iori's surveyors are arriving with compulsory relocation orders. His first useful task is to escort grain carts through a military checkpoint. Some villagers refuse to travel with him. Others accept because they are hungry.

The settlement becomes a recurring home: a market around a roofless bathhouse, a workshop built in a grounded ferry, a bell tower reachable by a ladder through its upper window. Progress is visible in ordinary things being repaired.

Ren initially proposes taking the records to the provincial court and confessing. Aya points out that the court condemned Hana after receiving exactly such a petition. Exposing the truth requires protecting the people who choose to speak, and surviving the response.

The act ends with Iori's relocation terms arriving. Families may receive provisions and passage if they accept new registration and renounce claims connected to the drowned villages. Some accept. Others refuse. The story respects that different people can make different choices about survival.

Ren chooses to help those who remain prepare an independent departure, carrying their own records and recovering the remains they can reach before the water returns.

## Act II: the price of a name

This is the broadest stretch of the game: connected valleys, hill roads, shrines, a reed-filled military camp, and the exposed streets of Ren's home village. A regional structure supports a substantial adventure without making an enormous open world a narrative requirement.

The chapters concern things people need done. Opening an alternative road keeps families from being separated at checkpoints. Recovering carpentry tools makes a broken crossing usable. Protecting a burial party gives people time to retrieve relatives. Escorting a willing witness matters because she has decided to testify, rather than because she is a container for a plot clue.

**Daigo's chapter** begins when Ren learns that an inspector is requisitioning a register of survivors' false identities for arrests. He reaches the waystation to intercept it, only to discover that Daigo has already substituted a false copy and hidden the original with the families it protects. Daigo privately tells him that several of those families want no part in a public petition. Ren agrees to respect that choice.

The inspector then arrives with Ren's arrest order. Daigo must visibly attempt the arrest or expose the entire route he has spent years protecting. He tells Ren, “If you go through my gate, my men have to know I couldn't stop you.” Their public duel is a dangerous bargain: Daigo fights hard enough to preserve his cover, and Ren must defeat him without killing him. After Ren escapes, Daigo can keep moving families under the inspector's nose. Their alliance depends on trust and restraint, rather than Ren's right to seize people's histories.

**Ema's chapter** forces Ren to oppose people he has wronged. She prepares a raid on the irrigation headworks. Aya agrees that the settlers need leverage but rejects an attack that would destroy the livelihoods of downstream villages. Ren must stop the raid and confront Ema at the waterworks. He defeats and wounds her while Aya's people prevent the destruction. Ema survives, furious and still convinced that petitions cannot win what armed power has taken. Her followers do not automatically forgive Ren or abandon their cause. He will continue encountering someone whose justified hatred he cannot resolve with a sword.

Afterward, Ren has a chance to meet some of the downstream families. They benefit from the reservoir but are neither Iori's accomplices nor faceless stakes in an argument. One of the people he helps here later brings boats to the evacuation.

**Masato's second duel** takes place on a mountain road. This time he is prepared for the habits Ren taught him. He has also found discrepancies in the execution order. Nevertheless, Ren has broken the law and killed during his flight; Masato will not pretend those acts disappear because his cause is sympathetic. Ren defeats him and again refuses to kill him. Masato does not immediately change sides. He returns to Iori to demand an answer.

At the midpoint, Iori offers Ren a private audience. He gives him a real chance to end the pursuit: surrender, accept sole responsibility for disobeying orders, and Iori will guarantee passage for Aya's immediate settlement under new identities. The wider claim will disappear with Ren.

Ren is tempted because this resembles the noble death he has been seeking. Aya refuses to let him decide for everyone. Sacrificing himself does not authorize him to bargain away their testimony or abandon the other settlements.

They choose the harder plan: complete a route over the old northern crossing, secure boats for its flooded approaches, and leave as a community whose members retain control over their names. Copies of the petition travel separately with willing witnesses and sympathetic traders. A single stolen document cannot settle the conflict or erase their work.

One copy reaches a royal circuit magistrate in the northern market town, beyond Iori's military jurisdiction. She agrees to hear the surviving petitioners under their original names. She can authorize an inquiry but has no force capable of opening the valley crossings herself. Iori contests the petition as a forgery and the witnesses as insurgents. Getting living people beyond his custody, where they can speak freely, becomes the concrete objective of the northern route. The outside court offers a chance at a hearing, not a guaranteed verdict.

## The dead on the road

The supernatural is real, restrained, and tied to the place.

At dusk, some of the drowned repeat fragments of their last journeys. A woman carries an invisible sleeping child. Soldiers stand in a rice field that used to be a barracks. Lights move beneath a stretch of water where there is no road on the present map.

The living disagree about what the apparitions are. Their consistent limit is that they repeat; they do not answer questions, predict events, or pronounce judgment. They cannot tell Ren whether he deserves forgiveness. Investigation still depends on living people, physical places, and records.

Most encounters are unsettling or sorrowful. A few are dangerous. The most important is **the Gatekeeper**, the drowned officer who held the original escape gate beside Ren. He attacks anyone approaching the crossing, repeating his final order. His fight introduces the place where Ren will eventually have to keep the road open.

Defeating him permits the engineers to reach the ruined mechanism. It does not free every ghost or imply that the massacre has been spiritually resolved. Afterward, his lantern remains on the wall, unlit.

Ren has no resurrection gift. Ordinary death and checkpoint retries can remain gameplay conventions. The story does not require an immortality mythology.

## Act III: the second flood

The rain begins.

Aya's preparations provide a credible evacuation route, but Iori controls its northern exit. The circuit magistrate's summons gives him a concrete problem: once the petitioners reach her, he cannot control their testimony. He could allow the departure and contest their claims in court. Instead, he chooses to keep them in custody until they sign retractions. A successful inquiry could expose the wartime flood and challenge estates founded on confiscated land. His claim to have delivered a blameless peace would collapse.

Iori declares the remaining settlements an armed uprising and orders the northern gate sealed. He offers transfer to guarded relocation camps only to those who surrender for screening and accept the revised registration. Passage to the magistrate is forbidden. The command creates a bottleneck as the approaches flood.

The danger is avoidable. Aya has shown how the reservoir can refill while everyone leaves. Iori's refusal makes the final conflict about his choices, rather than treating mass killing as an engineering necessity.

Earlier work pays off here. Hana's repaired crossings carry carts. Daigo guides families past patrols. The farmers' boats reach the lower approach. The witnesses and their copies have different routes. Ema and her followers protect families on the exposed causeway because those families still need them; she has not adopted Ren's politics. Aya runs the evacuation; Ren's task is to break the military blockade and hold the gate.

Masato witnesses soldiers receiving orders to turn civilian families back into rising water. He finally refuses a specific order, understanding what Ren should have done twelve years earlier. He takes a small group of soldiers to protect the crossing. He survives; rejecting Iori does not require a conveniently redemptive death.

Ren and Masato break through the outer guard post. Hana's crew raises the barrier, and Masato's soldiers take the winch room. Aya begins sending the evacuees through.

Iori brings his remaining loyal guard onto the upper terrace to retake the mechanism. The open road is making his officers choose publicly between their commander and the people below them. He cannot preserve control merely by riding away. Ren intercepts him while Masato holds the winch room.

## Final duel: the man who taught you

The fight takes place across the stone gatehouse terrace, with rain striking the flooded steps below. People are still crossing behind Ren. Their movement is part of the scene, rather than a fragile escort health bar.

Iori fights with the same fundamentals the player has learned: deliberate guard pressure, control of distance, and punishing premature attacks. He should feel like their most complete practitioner. His later phase is more exposed and forceful as he abandons the restraint he taught others.

Before the fight, Iori argues that admitting what happened will destroy the peace they built. Ren answers that the people below him have spent twelve years paying for it already.

Iori's last command is the same as his first:

“Close the gate.”

Ren refuses. The player wins a complete duel. Iori dies by Ren's hand, still trying to enforce the order. There is no absolving confession and no larger secret villain who made his choices for him.

The garrison breaks as Masato and the other dissenting officers hold the exit open. Hana and Aya bring the last evacuation groups through. Ren remains until the crossing is clear, then walks out himself.

The reservoir fills. The valley is lost again. This time, its remaining inhabitants have escaped together, and neither their history nor their claims can be submerged with it.

## Ending: remain to answer

The aftermath takes months. The refugees need shelter and fields. Some local officials recognize their old names; others resist. Iori's death has removed an immediate threat, not resolved ownership disputes or transformed the government into a just institution. The circuit court begins its inquiry with independent testimony, the surviving records, and evidence from officers who broke ranks. Powerful interests continue contesting the claims.

Ren submits a signed account of the original gate order. His own name appears among those responsible. He surrenders his sword and accepts custody for the inquiry. Saving the evacuation does not purchase immunity.

Aya gives testimony. She describes both what he did twelve years ago and what he did during the evacuation. She does not tell the court, or the player, how those facts ought to cancel each other out.

In the final scene, she visits him before the hearings begin. She tells him Hana has started building a school in the new settlement. Ren asks if they have named it.

“They are still arguing.”

For the first time, they share a small smile. Then the visiting bell sounds. Aya leaves. Ren remains to answer for what he did.

The final image is the new school doorway. Children measure their height against its frame. Hana has reused timber from her old house, including the piece she wore at her execution. Someone opens the door from inside.

One authored ending is the foundation. Optional stories can change who helps at the crossing, which families are reunited, and what appears in the new settlement. They should not turn responsibility into a good-or-evil score.

## Major encounters and their purpose

| Encounter | Why the opponent fights | Intended combat identity | What changes afterward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masato, shrine courtyard | Arrest Ren and prevent an unlawful escape | Clear fundamentals; a skilled pupil with readable commitments | Ren becomes a fugitive; Hana reaches the valley |
| Daigo, waystation | Preserve his cover under an inspector's arrest order | Patient defense, distance control, punishing impatience | Ren escapes without exposing Daigo's route; their trust becomes practical |
| Ema, irrigation headworks | Carry out retaliation she believes justice requires | Aggressive pressure and risky commitments around machinery | The raid is stopped; Ema survives as an unresolved political opponent |
| Masato, mountain road | Bring Ren to judgment while seeking an explanation from Iori | Familiar attacks with altered timing and counters to Ren's habits | Masato begins investigating on his own terms |
| The Gatekeeper, drowned crossing | Repeat the last order he obeyed in life | Relentless, recurring attack patterns that vary as ground floods | Engineers gain access to the northern crossing |
| Iori, gatehouse terrace | Preserve command by preventing a public breach of his order | Full command of the game's core sword mechanics | The blockade collapses; the evacuation can finish |

These are narrative and encounter intentions, not claims that the current prototype implements them. Nonlethal outcomes, multiple encounters, environmental changes, and persistent allies would need their own implementation decisions.

## How the present prototype can introduce the story

The existing courtyard duel is a natural foundation for Ren's confrontation with Masato. A first narrative slice can contain the execution order, Hana's short exchange with Ren, the duel, and a brief aftermath pointing toward the exposed valley. The larger adventure is not required to prove the opening works.

The prototype's existing guard, counter, posture, and stance systems already support the idea that these fighters learned related sword traditions. Story integration should preserve the established combat controls. Character identity should eventually come from original design and performance rather than the biography of a temporary character asset.

The current prototype ends duels lethally. The proposed opening would need a deliberate nonlethal victory resolution before it could accurately portray Masato's survival. Until that exists, the combat arena remains a mechanical prototype rather than an implemented story scene.

## Narrative principles

- Establish Ren's responsibility early and keep it concrete.
- Give people practical needs, pleasures, and disagreements beyond their suffering.
- Let survivors control whether they testify, reveal an identity, or forgive someone.
- Give major duels a present conflict and a consequence beyond access to the next area.
- Keep Iori's public achievements visible without treating his cruelty as necessary.
- Let the landscape reveal history; keep long explanations out of urgent combat scenes.
- Use the supernatural consistently and sparingly. It cannot settle the living characters' moral arguments.
- Preserve the difference between stopping immediate harm, repairing damage, and earning trust.
